Hospitals and insurers are now required to publish _machine-readable files_ listing the
price they've negotiated for every procedure with every payer. In theory, this is wonderful.
In practice the files are **enormous**: a single mid-sized payer's monthly drop weighs in
around `19,000,000,000` rows.[^1]

A naive load into Postgres is hopeless. A naive load into anything is hopeless. What I want
is to ask questions like _"how does this hospital's price for a knee replacement compare to
others in the same metro?"_ — and to do it on a laptop, in an afternoon.

## The structure of the problem

Most rows in these files are near-duplicates of other rows. A "rate" is keyed by a long
tuple _(payer, plan, network tier, billing code, modifier, place of service, provider group,
...)_, and the same negotiated price reappears under hundreds of slightly-different keys. If
we could collapse near-duplicate rate _bundles_ into canonical entries, we'd shrink the
working set by roughly two orders of magnitude.

Exact dedup misses this, because nothing is byte-identical. Edit distance is too expensive at
this scale. What we want is **approximate set similarity** — for every two bundles, a fast
estimate of _how much of one's contents are also in the other_.

### Enter MinHash

MinHash gives us exactly that, in a delightfully small amount of code. The idea: for each
set, compute a fixed-length _signature_ of _k_ minimum hash values. The probability that two
sets' signatures agree at any one position equals their Jaccard similarity. Comparing
signatures becomes O(_k_), independent of set size.

```python
def minhash(set_, k=128):
    sig = [float("inf")] * k
    for x in set_:
        for i in range(k):
            h = hash((i, x))
            if h < sig[i]: sig[i] = h
    return sig

def jaccard_estimate(a_sig, b_sig):
    return sum(a == b for a, b in zip(a_sig, b_sig)) / len(a_sig)
```

The production pipeline uses a variant called _bottom-K_ MinHash: instead of one min per
permutation, keep the _K_ smallest hashes overall. It's simpler, faster, and the estimator is
the same: the fraction of the K-smallest _union_ hashes that appear in both sets. The widget
below runs this for real on two 2,000,000-item sets, in your browser. Drag the sliders.[^2]

The thing to notice: even at K = 10,000 — a sketch that's a thousandth of a percent the size
of the data — the estimate is already within a couple of percentage points of the truth.
That's the bargain MinHash offers, and it's what makes the rest of the pipeline possible.

## Most files already live inside other files

Once you can cheaply estimate set similarity, the next thing you reach for is _set
containment_: for two files A and B, what fraction of A's rates also appear in B? Containment
is asymmetric, and it's exactly the question you want when deciding whether a file is
redundant.

At a 100% threshold (lossless) we already collapse seven files to four. Drop to 99% and we
keep two; at 95% it's just one file plus the tiny outlier `pl-299`, which is a different rate
universe entirely. Generalised across a payer's thousands of plan files, that's two orders of
magnitude of data we never had to load — and the only thing that made it visible was a cheap,
approximate similarity estimate over a 0.0001% sketch.

## From estimates to a working pipeline

Pairwise comparison of every bundle to every other bundle is still O(N²). The standard fix —
_locality-sensitive hashing_ over the MinHash signatures — lets us pull out candidate
near-duplicates in roughly linear time, then verify with the full Jaccard estimate.

> The combination of MinHash + LSH is one of those algorithmic ideas that, once you've seen
> it, you reach for constantly. It turns a quadratic problem into a near-linear one with about
> a page of code and no exotic data structures.

For the price-transparency project, this took the working set from _billions of rows we can't
load_ to _a few hundred million rate bundles we can keep in memory on a reasonable machine_.
Everything downstream — search, aggregation, the public UI — gets to assume the
canonicalisation step already happened.

## What I'd do differently

Two things, in retrospect. First: I underweighted how much of the win was in _picking the
right granularity for the "set"_. Hashing on raw rows is too noisy; hashing on bundles is much
better, but defining a bundle correctly took longer than implementing MinHash itself. Second:
I should have built the convergence visualisation a month earlier. Plotting estimated vs. true
Jaccard caught two off-by-one bugs in my signature code immediately.
